# Challenge: The Ten-Second Reset
*"For the next four weeks we're timing ourselves. Not to rush — to stop losing ten minutes a day to the in-between parts."*

**The problem.** The lesson isn't usually the problem. It's the four minutes at the start, the three at the end, and the ragged switch in the middle — small enough to shrug at individually, and somewhere between twenty and forty minutes a week once you actually count them. It's also where most low-level disruption starts, because an unstructured gap invites one.

**Season length:** 4 weeks · **Evidence pack:** `brain/evidence-packs/calmer-transitions.md`

**Why this works.** The pack's strongest card is the least exciting one: explicitly teaching, modeling, and practicing a transition routine — then running it *identically* every day — reliably cuts lost time and disruption. The active ingredient is sameness. Its second engine is pre-correction: a calm reminder delivered *just before* a predictable trouble spot, not after it goes wrong. This challenge is those two moves on a weekly rhythm. (The EEF rates behaviour interventions as moderate impact on moderate evidence — real, not miraculous.)

## The metric

**Minutes lost to one chosen transition, per week.** Pick the single rockiest moment in your day — entry, cleanup, the switch to stations, line-up — and time only that one, once a day, with your phone. Add up the week, or average it.

Time from the moment you give the signal to the moment the last student is doing the next thing. That end point matters: stopping the clock when the room goes quiet flatters the number and hides the actual problem, which is usually the gap after the noise ends and before the work starts.

**Class Pulse can't produce this one** — it's a stopwatch, not a gradebook. Type the week's number into chat, or write it at the top of the pulse file before you drop it in.

Set the target *with* the class in week 1. "We're at 3:40 and we want to finish under 2:00" is a real goal with a visible clock behind it. Ten seconds is the name of the challenge, not the target — say so, or the first week feels like failure.

## Week 1 — Baseline & launch

1. Time the transition for a few days **before changing anything, and without telling them you're timing it**. That average is the baseline — the AI records it in `my-classroom/dashboard.md`. Announcing it first gets you an unusually good week and a baseline you can't beat.
2. Run the launch: show the baseline, set the target together, and teach the routine like content — model it, walk through it, practice it once for real. Put the AI-generated step card where everyone can see it.
3. The AI generates the step-numbered one-pager, the matching slide, and the countdown timer slide.

## The weekly play pattern (weeks 2–4)

**Monday.** You give the AI this week's timing and say "run Monday." It updates the dashboard, shows the movement, and proposes this week's moves — each tagged to a pack card.

**Midweek — the one classroom move.** Rotate through the pack, one per week, never all at once:
- **The posted routine, unchanged** (teach-the-routine card): the same numbered steps, in the same order, every single day. The AI's job here is mostly to *not* redesign it. If you find yourself wanting a fresh version in week 3, that instinct is the thing the research warns about — stability is the intervention.
- **Pre-correct one minute before** (precorrection card): a short, neutral line in the slide notes for the moment *before* the switch — "in one minute we move: stand, push your chair in, walk to your number." Before, calmly. A warning barked after it's already loud is a different move with a different result.
- **Specific positives, well ahead of corrections** (praise-ratio card): the AI drafts true, specific lines you can say in the moment — "Table 3 was packed and standing in under ten seconds, that's exactly the reset I mean." Aim for roughly four positives to every correction. Generic "good job, class" doesn't count and students know it.
- **Shrink the gap itself** (channel-factors card): a visible countdown, one signal, and a next action to land on. One cue — not a chime *and* a hand *and* a countdown *and* your voice. Competing signals are their own kind of noise.

**Friday.** Two minutes: the AI drafts an aggregate callout for the slide ("2:10 average this week, down from 3:40") and one line goes into `my-classroom/class-story.md`.

## The finale

Week 4, the AI generates the season snapshot from `content-templates/season-snapshot.jsx` — baseline to finale, on the projector. Then the three-question retro, whole class: *What actually helped? What did we ignore? What should [AI name] do differently next season?* The answers go through the Feedback Cleaner into the class story. Then the deck comes back out.

Hit or miss, the season *ends*. A near-miss with a real retro beats a quietly abandoned streak — say that out loud to the class.

## Watch out

- **No clip charts, no color-coding, no collective punishment.** The pack calls public behavior color-coding a hard no: public shame, weak evidence, real harm. And "we'll all wait until everyone is quiet" penalizes the students who did the thing right and turns the room against whoever it lands on. The scoreboard is a clock. It measures the class, never a kid.
- **The boring version is the one that works.** Every instinct in week 3 will be to freshen it up — new steps, a new signal, a new chart. That instinct is the failure mode. If the number stalls, run the routine *more exactly*, not differently.
