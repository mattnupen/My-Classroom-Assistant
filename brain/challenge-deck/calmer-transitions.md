# Challenge: The Ten-Second Reset
*"We're timing ourselves. Not to rush, but to stop losing ten minutes a day to the in-between parts."*

**The problem.** The lesson usually isn't the problem. It's four minutes at the start, three at the end, and the ragged switch in the middle. Each is small enough to shrug off, but together they add up to twenty or forty minutes a week, and that's where most low-level disruption starts.

**Ask first:** "Which switch loses the most time, and about how long does it take now?"

**The number to watch. Minutes lost on **one** chosen transition (entry, cleanup, the switch to stations, line-up), timed once a day on your phone. Start the clock when you give the signal. Stop it when the last student is *doing the next thing*, not when the room goes quiet. On Monday, type each day's time into the chat ("3:40, 3:10, 4:05, 3:25"). Don't average them: separate days give the check enough numbers to go on. Class Pulse can't make this one.

**Evidence:** `brain/evidence-packs/calmer-transitions.md` (The strongest research is from K–5. With older students, the moves are sensible, but less studied.)

## Week 1: start now

Time the transition quietly once or twice before you teach the routine (tomorrow is fine), then start move 1 this week. Don't announce the timing first: that gets you one unusually good day and a starting number you can't beat. With fewer than three starting times, the first check is a rough read (say so at the check). Then show the class and set a target together ("we're at 3:40; let's get under 2:00"). "Ten seconds" is the name of the challenge, not the target. Say so.

## The moves, one at a time (about two weeks each)

1. **Teach the routine like content.** Three to five numbered steps, modeled and practiced once for real, then kept exactly the same every day. *Strong research behind this for K–5 (WWC practice guide, 2024).*
2. **A calm reminder one minute before.** "In one minute we move: stand, push your chair in, walk to your number." Say it before the switch, not after it gets loud. *Strong research behind this for K–5 (WWC practice guide, 2024).*
3. **Name what's going right.** Specific lines the teacher says during the transition ("Table 3 was ready in ten seconds"). More praise than correction helps, but there's no magic ratio. Praise individuals quietly. *Strong research behind this for K–5 (WWC practice guide, 2024).*
4. **Time it and show the class its time.** A countdown slide, one signal, a next task to land on, and today's time on the board. *Promising, less proven: small single-class studies (Yarbrough et al., 2004).*

## Watch out

- **No clip charts, no color charts, no punishing the whole class.** The scoreboard is a clock. It measures the class, never a kid.
- **The boring version is the one that works.** When the number stalls, run the same routine *more exactly*. Don't redesign it.
