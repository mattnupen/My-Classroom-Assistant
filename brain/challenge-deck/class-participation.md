# Challenge: The Whole Room Talks
*"For the next four weeks I'm counting how many different people say something out loud. Not how smart it is. How many."*

**The problem.** Six kids carry every discussion and everyone knows it — including the other eighteen, who have quietly agreed that talking is somebody else's job. It isn't shyness so much as a settled arrangement, and it holds until something changes the cost of speaking.

**Season length:** 4 weeks · **Evidence pack:** `brain/evidence-packs/class-participation.md`

**Why this works.** The pack's strongest card is the oldest: Rowe found that extending the pause after a question past about three seconds made student responses **300–700% longer**, cut "I don't know," and pulled in students who hadn't been talking. Most teachers wait under a second. The second engine is rehearsal — letting students say their answer to one partner before anyone says it to the room; oral-language approaches run about **+6 months** in the EEF toolkit. This challenge is those two moves on a weekly rhythm.

## The metric

**How many different students spoke in the whole-class portion of one chosen lesson each week.** Breadth, not volume — a tally of distinct voices, not a count of comments.

The practical version: pick one lesson a week, keep the roster on a clipboard or a sticky note, and put a mark by a name the first time that student says anything to the room. Count the marks at the end. That's your number.

**Class Pulse can't produce this one** — nothing in a gradebook knows who talked. It's a tally, and it takes about as long as taking attendance. Type it into chat, or write it at the top of the pulse file before you drop it in.

Set the target *with* the class in week 1, after they've seen the baseline. "Eleven of us talked on Tuesday; let's finish the season above eighteen" is a real goal that leaves room for a bad day. Telling them everyone will talk every day is a promise the room can't keep.

**What this number does and doesn't tell you.** It measures how many people are in the conversation, which is exactly the thing this challenge is trying to change. It says nothing about whether what they said was any good — deliberately. The moment the count starts rewarding quality, the students who don't speak because they're scared of being wrong have a new reason not to speak.

## Week 1 — Baseline & launch

1. Run the tally on one normal lesson **before changing anything**. Don't announce it that day. That number is the baseline — the AI records it in `my-classroom/dashboard.md`.
2. Run the launch: tell them what you counted and what you got. Be straight about it — "eleven of twenty-four, and I want to see that number climb" lands better than any pep talk. Set the target together, or put it on the ballot with `content-templates/student-voting-form.md`.
3. The AI generates the kickoff slide and the first week's question set with three-second pause cues written into the speaker notes.

## The weekly play pattern (weeks 2–4)

**Monday.** You give the AI this week's tally and say "run Monday." It updates the dashboard, shows the movement, and proposes this week's moves — each tagged to a pack card.

**Midweek — the one classroom move.** Rotate through the pack, one per week, never all at once:
- **Three seconds of silence** (wait-time card): the AI writes open questions worth waiting for and puts "(pause — count to three)" directly in the slide notes. This is a habit only you can perform — the AI can cue the pause, it can't hold it. Holding a real three-second silence is harder than it sounds and it is the single highest-value thing in this pack.
- **Rehearse with a partner first** (oral-language card): every prompt structured think → pair → share, with a task and a timer. Then ask for *your partner's* best point — it lowers the stakes again, because you're reporting rather than claiming.
- **Warm, predictable cold-calling** (cold-call card): pair a name with a question that student has *just rehearsed with a partner*, in a warm tone, always with a way back in ("totally fine to say 'still thinking' — I'll come back to you"). Never run this card before the rehearsal card. Cold-calling on unrehearsed material teaches fear, and the pack says so.
- **Name the norm, never the silence** (social-norming card): "almost everyone got an idea on the table yesterday — that's just who we are in here." True majority only. If participation was thin that day, the AI switches to an invitation instead of inventing a norm.

**Friday.** Two minutes: the AI drafts an aggregate callout for the slide ("nineteen different people talked this week") and one line goes into `my-classroom/class-story.md`.

## The finale

Week 4, the AI generates the season snapshot from `content-templates/season-snapshot.jsx` — baseline to finale, on the projector. Then the three-question retro, whole class: *What actually helped? What did we ignore? What should [AI name] do differently next season?* Run this one as an anonymous write-down through the Feedback Cleaner rather than a discussion — asking quiet students to speak up about why they don't speak up is a trap.

Hit or miss, the season *ends*. A near-miss with a real retro beats a quietly abandoned streak — say that out loud to the class.

## Watch out

- **No participation tracker on the wall, and no points for talking.** A public tally of who speaks is a shame board for the students this challenge exists to reach — the pack rules it out flatly. Points and prizes carry the overjustification risk: pay students to talk and talking becomes a transaction rather than something worth doing. Your clipboard tally is *yours*; the class only ever sees the total.
- **Cold-calling without the rehearsal is the way this goes wrong.** It's the fastest-looking move in the pack and the one that backfires hardest on its own. If you only run one card this season, run wait time.
