# Research Foundations

The research behind `teaching-principles.md`: who did the work, what they found, and how strong it is. It uses the same strength labels as everything else (defined in `evidence-engine.md`). You don't need to read this; your AI follows the principles either way. It's here so anyone can check.

The research for your specific challenge lives in the evidence packs. This file is the base every classroom shares.

*Last checked against sources: 2026-09-22.*

---

| Principle | Research | How strong |
|---|---|---|
| **Wise feedback** (2): critique that comes with high standards and belief the student can meet them | Cohen, Steele & Ross (1999); Yeager et al. (2014). In randomized studies with 7th graders, adding one sentence ("I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them") made students much more likely to revise their essays. The effect was largest for Black students who distrusted school. | Promising, less proven: small studies, not yet widely repeated |
| **If-then plans** (3): tie a goal to a specific time and place | Gollwitzer (1999); Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), meta-analysis of 94 tests: a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d ≈ 0.65), and on getting started (d ≈ 0.61) | Good research, with limits: mostly adults and college students |
| **Identity-based motivation** (4): school goals that feel like part of who students already are | Oyserman, Bybee & Terry (2006): a short in-school program with 8th graders raised grades and test scores and cut absences in a randomized trial, with effects lasting two years | Good research, with limits: few trials |
| **Showing movement** (5): talk about direction, not just status | A practical idea drawn from goal-setting and feedback research, not a tested classroom intervention | A practical idea, not tested research |
| **Describe what most people do, not what's wrong** (6) | Cialdini (2003); Schultz et al. (2007). Strong outside schools (energy use, littering). In schools, telling families the plain facts worked without comparing them to classmates (Rogers & Feller, 2018), and perfect-attendance awards backfired by suggesting good attendance was unusual (Robinson et al., 2019). | Promising, less proven in classrooms |
| **Celebrate publicly, correct privately** (7) | O'Leary et al. (1970): quiet reprimands only the student could hear cut disruption more than loud ones. Schneider et al. (2021): most of 511 middle schoolers preferred to be praised quietly, so individual praise is quiet by default. | Promising, less proven: small or survey studies, consistent with WWC guidance on acknowledging expected behavior |
| **Anyone can grow** (8): praise effort and strategy, not "smart" | Mueller & Dweck (1998): praising intelligence reduced children's persistence after failure. But school-wide growth-mindset programs have small effects (Sisk et al., 2018, meta-analysis), and a national trial found gains mainly for lower-achieving students in supportive schools (Yeager et al., 2019). | Good research, with limits: the effects are real but small |
| **Small next steps** (9): an action someone could start in the next ten minutes | Bandura & Schunk (1981), near goals with children; Leventhal, Singer & Jones (1965), concrete plans turn intentions into action | Promising, less proven: small, older studies |
| **Belonging** (10): signals that "you belong here" and early struggle is normal | Walton & Cohen (2011); a 22-college trial found it works only where students actually have chances to belong (Walton et al., 2023); a middle-school version raised attendance in one district (Borman et al., 2019), but a repeat in another district didn't (Borman et al., 2025) | Good research, with limits: strongest in college; mixed in K–12 |

## If you want to read one thing

David Yeager, *10 to 25: The Science of Motivating Young People* (2024). It's readable, and it ties most of this together for middle and high school: high standards plus real support. For the honest limits of growth mindset, read Carol Dweck's own 2015 *Education Week* commentary, ["Carol Dweck Revisits the 'Growth Mindset'"](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-carol-dweck-revisits-the-growth-mindset/2015/09).

## What isn't proven

- **Having students name and shape their AI.** No studies exist yet. It borrows from research on student voice and ownership, but the specific idea is new.
- **The two-week check-and-decide loop** (`challenge-cycle.md`). It's a careful, simple routine for one class. It isn't a research method, and one class's numbers can't prove a strategy works.
- **The offline apps.** They're about privacy and saving time, not teaching.

If you try this and learn something, share it. The field is small enough that one careful teacher's report matters.
