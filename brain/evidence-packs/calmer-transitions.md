# Evidence Pack: Calmer Transitions and Fewer Disruptions

**Goal:** Smoother starts, stops, and switches, with less low-level disruption.
**Checked:** 2026-09-22 (first built 2026-06-14; now grounded in the 2024 WWC practice guide; the "4:1 praise ratio" claim corrected)
**Number to watch for this goal:** minutes lost on one chosen transition, timed once a day. Keep each day's time as its own number (don't average them), so the check in `../challenge-cycle.md` has enough numbers.

> The cards are in the order to try them, **one at a time** (see `../challenge-cycle.md`). Strength labels are defined in `../evidence-engine.md`.
>
> **Who was studied.** The strongest evidence here comes from K–5 classrooms. For middle and high school, the same moves are sensible, but there's less direct research. Say so when the class is older. The EEF rates behavior programs as moderate impact on moderate evidence, and warns that studies run by the people selling a program tend to show bigger effects.

---

### 1. Teach the routine like content: model it, practice it, keep it the same

- **How strong:** Strong research behind this (K–5) · **Source:** WWC practice guide, *Teacher-Delivered Behavioral Interventions in Grades K–5* (2024): "co-establish, model, and teach clear expectations," rated strong · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Clear expectations that are taught, modeled, and practiced (not just posted) reduce problem behavior. The routine has to run the same way every day until it's automatic.

**The move.** A numbered, step-by-step card and a matching slide for the one rocky transition. Model it, practice it once for real, and then don't change it.

**The number to watch.** Minutes lost on that transition.

**Worked example.**
> **Entry: first 3 minutes, every day**
> 1. Phone in the caddy.
> 2. Warm-up's on the board. Start it.
> 3. Pencil moving before the timer hits zero.

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* the same steps every day, posted and practiced. *Badly:* explained once, or redesigned every week.

**Watch out.** Keeping it the same is what makes it work. Don't refresh it in week 3.

---

### 2. A calm reminder just before the hard moment

- **How strong:** Strong research behind this (K–5) · **Source:** WWC practice guide (2024): "remind students to engage in expected behaviors," rated strong; Colvin, Sugai, Good & Lee (1997) · **Who was studied:** K–5; Colvin et al. was one elementary school's hallway, cafeteria, and dismissal transitions · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** A short, warm reminder of what to do, given *before* a predictable trouble spot, reduces problem behavior. In the 1997 study, adding reminders and active supervision cut problem behavior in all three transition settings.

**The move.** A one-line reminder in the slide notes for the minute before the switch.

**The number to watch.** Minutes lost on that transition.

**Worked example.**
> [Before stations: "In one minute we move. That looks like: stand, push your chair in, walk to your number. Here we go."]

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* before the problem, calm, specific. *Badly:* a warning barked once it's already loud.

**Watch out.** It's a reminder, not a threat. The tone decides whether it works.

---

### 3. Notice and name what's going right, often

- **How strong:** Strong research behind this (K–5) · **Source:** WWC practice guide (2024): "acknowledge students for demonstrating expected behaviors," rated strong; Caldarella et al. (2020) · **Who was studied:** K–5; Caldarella et al. observed 151 elementary classrooms · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Specific acknowledgment of expected behavior reduces problem behavior. In the 151 classrooms, the higher the ratio of praise to reprimands, the more time students spent on task. But there was **no magic ratio**. The often-quoted "4 to 1" isn't a threshold that research supports; more is simply better.

**The move.** Draft true, specific lines the teacher can say during the transition. Keep class-facing notes about what's working.

**The number to watch.** Minutes lost on that transition.

**Worked example.**
> "Table 3 was packed up and standing in under ten seconds. That's exactly the reset I mean."

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* specific, true, frequent. *Badly:* a generic "good job, class."

**Watch out.** In a survey of 511 middle schoolers, most wanted praise delivered quietly (Schneider et al., 2021). Praise the class or a table out loud; praise individuals quietly.

---

### 4. Time it, and show the class its time

- **How strong:** Promising, less proven · **Source:** Yarbrough et al. (2004), the "Timely Transitions Game"; Hine, Ardoin & Foster (2015) · **Who was studied:** single elementary classrooms, in small studies · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Timing a transition, showing the class its time afterwards, and having one clear signal cut transition times in small elementary studies. Those studies also used a small class reward. This version keeps the timer and the posted time, which a teacher can run tomorrow.

**The move.** A countdown slide, one signal, one "what to do the second you're done" line, and the class's time written on the board afterwards.

**The number to watch.** Minutes lost on that transition. Here the number is also the move.

**Worked example.**
> [Slide: 2:00 countdown. "Pack up, then start the exit ticket." One chime at zero. Afterwards: "Today: 1:45. Best so far: 1:30."]

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* one cue, a next action to land on, the class's time shown. *Badly:* a chime *and* a hand signal *and* a countdown *and* your voice.

**Watch out.** It's the class's time, never an individual's. No waiting on "whoever's still talking."

---

## Handle with care

- **Clip charts and public color charts.** Public shame and no good evidence. Never.
- **Punishing the whole class.** It penalizes the students who did it right. There's no support for it.
- **Vendor behavior programs.** Look for independent evaluations; the EEF warns that studies run by the program's sellers overstate effects.
