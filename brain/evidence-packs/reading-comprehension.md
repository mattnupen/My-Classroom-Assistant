# Evidence Pack: Building Reading Comprehension

**Goal:** Students understand more of what they read, not just decode the words.
**Checked:** 2026-09-22 (first built 2026-06-14; added the WWC ratings, the reciprocal-teaching effect sizes, and an honest section on how to tell if it's working)
**Number to watch for this goal:** see "How to tell if it's working" at the end. The short version: a reading test won't show much change within a season, so we count how many groups use the strategies on their own.

> The cards are in the order to try them, **one at a time** (see `../challenge-cycle.md`). Strength labels are defined in `../evidence-engine.md`.

---

### 1. Teach a few comprehension strategies, explicitly

- **How strong:** Strong research behind this · **Source:** EEF, *Reading comprehension strategies* (184 studies); WWC practice guide, *Improving Adolescent Literacy* (2008): "direct and explicit comprehension strategy instruction," rated strong · **Effect:** about +7 months (EEF) · **Who was studied:** primary and secondary; the WWC guide covers grades 4–12 · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Explicitly teaching and modeling a few strategies (predicting, questioning, clarifying, summarizing) raises comprehension. The EEF rates its evidence *moderate*, not high: many of the studies were run by the people who designed the approach, and results vary a lot from study to study. What matters is students using the strategies on their own, and the teacher has to plan how to hand over that control.

**The move.** Name the same three or four strategies. Model them aloud on a real text. Reuse the same prompt card every week until students don't need it.

**The number to watch.** Pairs or groups using a strategy without being prompted, in one reading lesson a week (see the end of this pack).

**Worked example.**
> **While you read, keep asking:**
> • *Predict:* what comes next, and what makes me think so?
> • *Clarify:* what's one word or sentence I should reread?
> • *Summarize:* what's the gist so far, in one sentence?

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* a few strategies, modeled, reused until they're automatic. *Badly:* a new strategy every week, or a worksheet students fill in to hand to the teacher.

**Watch out.** More strategies isn't better. The benefit comes from repetition, not variety.

### 2. Reciprocal teaching: students take turns leading

- **How strong:** Good research, with limits · **Source:** Palincsar & Brown (1984); Rosenshine & Meister (1994), review of 16 studies · **Effect:** median effect 0.32 on standardized tests; 0.88 on tests the researchers wrote themselves · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Small groups take turns leading the four strategies on a shared text. The teacher models first, then fades out. The gains are real, but much smaller on standardized tests than on the researchers' own tests. Expect modest gains, not the big numbers from the original studies.

**The move.** Four role cards, a one-page how-to, and a teacher script for the first few sessions that gets shorter each time.

**The number to watch.** Groups running their roles without the teacher stepping in, in one lesson a week.

**Worked example.**
> **Questioner card.** After your group reads the section, ask two questions: one whose answer is right there in the text, and one that makes the group think past it ("Why do you think she lied?").

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* modeled a few times, then run by the students. *Badly:* the teacher leads every turn, and it becomes a read-aloud worksheet.

**Watch out.** Don't hand out the role cards cold. Students need to see each role modeled first.

### 3. Discuss the text, with reasons and evidence

- **How strong:** Good research, with limits · **Source:** WWC, *Improving Adolescent Literacy* (grades 4–12): "extended discussion of text meaning," rated moderate; EEF, *Oral language interventions* (about +6 months) · **Limits:** the most direct evidence (WWC, grades 4–12) is rated moderate; the broader EEF oral-language evidence is strong but mostly from younger pupils · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Structured, purposeful talk about a text improves comprehension. It works best when it's tied to what the class is reading and pushes students to give reasons and point to lines in the text, not just recall what happened.

**The move.** Questions with several defensible answers, set up as think, then pair, then share, so everyone says their answer once to a partner first.

**The number to watch.** Pairs citing a line from the text without being prompted, in one lesson a week.

**Worked example.**
> **Think** (1 min): Was the narrator fair to her sister? Find one line that backs you up.
> **Pair:** trade your lines. Did you pick the same moment?
> **Share:** did anyone's partner change their mind?

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* real disagreement is possible, and every answer points to the text. *Badly:* questions with one right answer, or jumping straight to hands up.

**Watch out.** Questions with a single right answer shut discussion down.

### 4. Teach the words and background the text assumes

- **How strong:** Strong research behind this (for teaching vocabulary) · **Source:** WWC, *Improving Adolescent Literacy*: "explicit vocabulary instruction," rated strong; Recht & Leslie (1988) on background knowledge · **Who was studied:** grades 4–12 (WWC); 64 seventh and eighth graders (Recht & Leslie) · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

**What the research found.** Explicitly teaching the key words in a text helps comprehension. Separately, in Recht & Leslie's small study, weaker readers who knew about baseball understood and remembered a baseball passage as well as strong readers, while strong readers who didn't know baseball did worse. What a text quietly assumes you know matters a lot.

**The move.** Before a hard text, a short "before you read" note: three or four key words, plus one paragraph on exactly what the text assumes you already know.

**The number to watch.** The same weekly count as the move you're running. This card supports the others.

**Worked example.**
> **Before you read, three things that'll help:** *Rationing* = limits on how much you can buy. In the war, families got coupons for sugar and gas. Keep that in mind when the family argues at dinner.

**Done well vs. done badly.** *Well:* short, and aimed at exactly what this text assumes. *Badly:* a two-page summary that replaces the reading.

**Watch out.** If the note does the understanding for them, there's nothing left for them to do.

## How to tell if it's working (the honest version)

**A reading test won't show you much change within a season.** Comprehension grows slowly. The most common quick weekly check for older readers, the "maze" (students choose the right word at every seventh-word blank, for two or three minutes), grows slowly: studies find anywhere from about 0.2 to 2 correct answers a week (Chung, Espin & Stevenson, 2018, who studied Dutch seventh graders on a two-minute maze, sum up the range). Scores also rise with normal growth whether or not a strategy is working, so over two to six weeks a class maze score can't tell you whether one strategy helped. The maze also leans more on decoding and fluency than on deeper understanding (Muijselaar et al., 2017), and different passages vary in difficulty, which adds noise. So a weekly test number would mostly show noise, and could lead to dropping a good strategy too early.

**So the number to watch is whether students are doing the strategies on their own.** That's what the research says the gains depend on, and it can change within weeks. In one reading lesson a week, during the pair or group part, count how many pairs or groups use the strategy without being prompted (predict, question, clarify, summarize, or cite a line). Report it as, for example, "5 of 8 groups." It takes no extra class time. This measure is **a practical idea, not tested research**, and you should say so.

**Optional: a reading check at the start and end of the year** (not every week). If the teacher wants a test number, suggest a three-minute maze at the start and end of the year, using passages at the same level from the same source. You can make them: keep the first sentence intact, then replace every seventh word with three choices. Score the number correct. Read the change as a rough sign, and don't use it to judge a single strategy.

## Handle with care

- **Round-robin or "popcorn" reading.** Little comprehension benefit, and most students tune out while they wait their turn.
- **Reading logs that only count minutes.** They turn reading into a chore unless there's a strategy or a real reason to read.
- **Matching texts to "learning styles."** No support (see `../evidence-engine.md`).
